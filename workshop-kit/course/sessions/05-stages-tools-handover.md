# Session 5 — Stages you can step into, steady parts into a tool, hand it on

Jake's module three. The attendee splits their job into stages that each leave one file,
moves one exact step into a tool, clears two versions of one fact, hands the folder to a
stranger with the assistant off, swaps the model, and runs the walk test.

## 1. The principle

Split the job into stages that each leave one file, put the exact steps in a tool, keep one
home per fact, and the folder outlives you and the model.

## 2. Concepts

### Start from a cord

In some factories a cord runs the length of the line. Any worker who sees a fault pulls it,
and the line stops until the fault is fixed where it started. The practice is usually told as
Toyota's, under the name jidoka. The details differ by teller. The idea is the same in every
telling: stop early, where the fix is cheap, and let the person who saw the fault be the one
who stops the line.

The assistant's work needs the same cord. A job that runs from one prompt to a finished
result leaves you one place to intervene, at the end, where every fix is expensive. Jake calls
that the narrow funnel. The alternative is to split the job into stages that each leave one
file, and to put your eyes where a wrong thing is still cheap to fix.

### What is beneath the stage

Go back once more. In 1801 Joseph Marie Jacquard showed a loom controlled by punched cards.
Jake's video says 1804. His written page corrects it to 1801 on the Computer History Museum's
dating, and the course takes the page. The card is the first instruction that a human could
inspect before the machine ran it, and the first that could be wrong in a way a human could
see. A portrait woven on such a loom in 1839 is said to have needed some twenty-four thousand
cards, a figure the page supports.

Everything since has worked the same way: an instruction written somewhere a person can read
it, then run by a machine that does not understand it. Every layer of that stack was
unreliable when new and became dependable through checks. The 1947 moth in a relay is the
famous case, and the page corrects the story: engineers already said "bug", the moth was a
memorable literal one. A chip's division fault in 1994 cost its maker a reported charge of
hundreds of millions, as a replacement accounting charge and not per bad sum. A 2003 Belgian
election count gained a few thousand votes that a recount removed, and the parliamentary
record calls a spontaneous bit flip the probable cause without naming a cosmic ray. The
pattern in each: name the failure, add a check, test the check. The assistant is the newest
layer and is in its moth-in-the-relay years. The answer is not to trust it or to refuse it. The
answer is the check.

### One stage, one file

Each stage of the job writes one plain file, and that file is where you look. Jake's animation
folder runs voice, timed words, storyboard, scene, render. His map carries one rule: nothing
advances until a person has looked at the previous file. In one of his recorded sessions the
contract for each stage has four headings, and the course adopts them:

- **Input:** the files this stage reads, named.
- **Do:** the task, in plain words.
- **Output:** the one file it writes, and where.
- **Human check:** what a person looks at before the next stage runs.

Put the check where the error starts, before it spreads. Jake reviews a transcript before it
is copied into six outputs, because a misheard name surfaces there first. His own course
carries the worked example: a script that said two years where the fact was close to four, and
the fix was one line, because it was caught at the script.

### Three strengths of stop

A line in the map is the weakest stop. Jake says it is nearly always followed, and that is why
it is not enough for anything that sends, deletes or spends. The tool's permission mode is the
second: ask before every edit or run, or plan only. A hook is the third: a rule in the tool's
settings that blocks an action whatever the map says. Choose by cost of the mistake. A draft
that lands in the wrong folder needs the first. A send needs the third.

### Where to put your eyes

Do not watch the stages that are scripted. Watch the ones where taste changes the result.
Stepping in is richer than approve or reject: add a line, stretch a beat, and most often,
delete. The paper behind the course reports where thirty-three practitioners said they edit,
and the shape is a U: heavy at the first stage, where direction is set, light in the middle,
heavy again at the end, where the output is checked against earlier decisions. The paper says
plainly that the figure is self-report from conversations, not measurement. The course repeats
the hedge. Use the U as a question: where in your job is the direction set, and where is the
end checked against it?

### The steady parts into a tool

Anything that must give the same answer from the same input belongs in a tool, not in the
assistant's judgment. Timing words. Totalling a column. Counting bookings in a week. Jake's test
for a candidate: a step you have done the same way three or four times, whose right answer does
not depend on taste. Ask the assistant to write the script, put it in a tools folder, and check
it once, properly, against a result you made by hand. A wrong script is wrong the same way
every time, so one honest check is worth more than many glances.

One input, one output. The tool does its step and stops. It never starts the next stage. That
keeps it swappable, and it keeps the folder, not the script, as the thing that says what
happens next. McIlroy's rule from 1978 again: one thing well. The story usually told about
Donald Knuth and McIlroy in 1986, a long bespoke program answered by a short chain of existing
tools, is the same rule as a parable. Reuse before you build.

### A third pile between the two

One of Jake's recent videos adds a pile between judgment and exact answer. Some steps are
neither a paragraph nor a sum. They are a pick from a list, a yes or no, a rating, and they
repeat. Those are sorting decisions. A chat model does them slowly and inconsistently. A small
sorter does them fast with a confidence number. Jake's rule of thumb, from a product launch and
hedged by the library as vendor context: above a high confidence, act. Below a low one, hand to
a person. In between, act and flag. Always leave an "other" pile, because the sorter cannot
invent a category you did not list. His phrase for the whole arrangement: the model gives an
opinion, the code makes the decision. And the decision rules live in a file in the folder, so
that an overrule by a person becomes a test case for the next version.

The three-way test for any step: if the output is a paragraph, the assistant. If it is a pick
from a list and it repeats, a sorter. If it has one exact answer, code.

### The swamp

The assistant makes files cheap, and a folder that was clean in week one is a swamp by week
six: drafts, versions, summaries of summaries. Then the assistant finds three prices and picks
the stale one. Jake's three checks:

1. The fresh-session test. A new session must say what the folder is, what is in progress
   and what is next. If it cannot, the map is out of date.
2. One home per fact. Fix the voice file and every job picks it up.
3. Clear things out. Old work goes to an archive that the map marks as never read. Delete
   freely what can be regenerated. Leave originals, sources and other people's files alone,
   with the permission mode as your guard.

Jake admits in one recording that his own files had drifted: an overview that still said one
format while the map said another. The method does not prevent drift. The checks catch it.

### Fix the cause, not the output

The paper's last section argues the point from programming: editing the output fixes this
run, editing the source fixes every future run. When you tighten the same opening paragraph
three runs running, that is not editing. That is a diagnosis. The contract should say "opening
under three sentences". When the tone drifts formal every time, the voice file needs a
stronger example. Recurring edits are debugging information. Some edits stay edits: a turn of
phrase no rule would have produced. Learn to tell the two apart.

### Hand it on

The map is the handover document, for the next person as much as for the assistant. Jake's
test: could you do the work with the assistant switched off, from the map and the contracts?
Item 10 of the walk test asks a stranger to do exactly that. Two ways to hand over: give a copy,
which suits a template, or work in one shared place, which needs agreement on who changes
what. Access is a permissions question the folder does not settle.

Then the model swap. Jake's bet, and the paper's, is that files and folders outlast any model
or vendor. The tree has survived every platform change since Ritchie and Thompson's 1974 paper
described Unix. Swapping models means pointing the new one at the same folder and perhaps
renaming the map file. Item 11 of the walk test runs it. The paper itself notes that all its
testing was on one model family and that cross-model results were not shown, so run the item,
and do not assume it.

### The rules I run on

Gates are human checkboxes. A gate is a line a person reads and a box a person ticks. The
assistant reads the gate and never ticks it. Mine sit at the top of a pull request, and I tick
them from my phone or not at all (why: a gate is where my judgement enters, and one ticked
unread is no gate · 2026-10-14).

Never build an orchestrator. Session 3 said it. Today you are tempted again, because you now
have stages and a script, and a script that runs the stages in order looks like progress. It
hides the contracts it replaces. The folder is the pipeline (why: a script that drives the
stages hides the contracts it replaces · 2026-10-14).

One home per fact, and every rule carries its why and its date. Both are items in the walk
test now. They were taught in sessions 2 and 4. Today the folder proves them.

### Three-line exercise

Write three lines about your folder:

1. The stage where your judgment changes the result most.
2. The one step you will move into a tool first.
3. The fact you will search for in walk test item 4.

## 3. Activities

The attendee's sheets are in `../activities/`.

### A5.1 Stages with a human check (25 min) — sheet `s5-a1-stages-with-a-check.md`

Alone, then pairs. The attendee writes their job as stages, each with the four-heading
contract, and marks the one or two stages where their judgment changes the result.

- **Worked:** each stage names one output file. Each contract has a Human check. The check
  for the first stage sits where errors start. The marked stages are the ones with taste in
  them, not the scripted ones.
- **Common failure:** every stage is marked for review. Ask: "Which of these would you not
  look at if you had five minutes?" Unmark those.

### A5.2 One steady step into a tool (25 min) — sheet `s5-a2-steady-step-into-a-tool.md`

Alone. The attendee picks one step that passes the candidate test, asks the assistant for a
script in a tools folder with one input and one output, checks it once against a hand-made
result, and adds one line to the map saying when it runs.

- **Worked:** the script has one input and one output and stops. The hand-made result and
  the script's result match, or the attendee can name the difference. The map line says when
  it runs, not what runs next.
- **Common failure:** the script does the step and then "moves on to the next stage". Ask:
  "Which contract did that line replace?" Cut the line.

### A5.3 Two versions of one fact (15 min) — sheet `s5-a3-two-versions-of-one-fact.md`

Pairs, on the example's messy folder. The fourth scenario: the two price lists. The pair finds
the one home, moves the other to an archive, and writes the archive rule into the map.

- **Worked:** one price file remains in the working folder. The archive exists and the map
  says it is never read. A fresh request uses the right price.
- **Common failure:** the pair merges the two lists into a third file and keeps all three.
  Ask: "How many homes does the price have now?"

### A5.4 Handover with the assistant off (20 min) — sheet `s5-a4-handover.md`

Pairs swap folders. Each attendee does one request from their pair's folder with the
assistant off, from the map and the contracts alone. Questions they had to ask go on a list.

- **Worked:** the request was done by hand from the map. The question list is short and
  each question becomes a line in the map.
- **Common failure:** the stranger asks where the output goes. Ask the owner: "Which line of
  the map should have said?" Write it.

### A5.5 The model swap (10 min) — sheet `s5-a5-model-swap.md`

Alone. The attendee points a second model or a second tool at their folder, renames or
copies the map if the tool reads another name, and repeats walk test item 2.

- **Worked:** the same files open and the output lands in the same place. The only change was
  the map's file name.
- **Common failure:** a contract had to change for the new tool. Ask: "What did the contract
  say that only one tool understands?" Rewrite it in plain words.

### A5.6 The walk test (20 min) — sheet `s5-a6-walk-test.md`

Pairs. Each attendee runs all eleven items of `../walk-test.md` on their own folder, with
their pair recording pass or fail and the evidence.

- **Worked:** every item has a pass or a fail with evidence. Each fail names its fix.
- **Common failure:** an item is marked pass without evidence. Ask: "What did you see?"

## 4. Story slot

> **Story slot (10 min).** One story from the facilitator's own practice. It must carry: a
> gate that was ticked without being read, or a script that quietly took a decision, and what
> it cost. The point to land: the check is only a check if a person does it.

## 6. Fiche — session 5, the attendee keeps this page

**The principle.** Split the job into stages that each leave one file, put the exact steps in
a tool, keep one home per fact, and the folder outlives you and the model.

**One stage, one file.** Each stage writes one plain file. That file is where you look.

**The contract, four headings.** Input, the files it reads. Do, the task. Output, the one file
it writes. Human check, what a person looks at before the next stage runs.

**Put the check where the error starts.** Before it is copied anywhere.

**Three strengths of stop.** A line in the map, usually followed. The permission mode, asks
first. A hook, blocks whatever the map says. A send needs the third.

**Watch where taste changes the result.** Not the scripted steps. Stepping in means add,
stretch, and most often delete.

**Steady parts into a tool.** Done the same way three or four times, no taste in the answer.
One input, one output, stops. Check it once against a hand-made result. It never starts the
next stage.

**The three-way test.** A paragraph, the assistant. A pick from a list that repeats, a sorter
with an "other" pile. One exact answer, code.

**The swamp.** Fresh-session test. One home per fact. Archive, marked never read. Delete what
can be regenerated. Never originals.

**Fix the cause.** The same edit three runs running is a diagnosis. Change the contract or
the voice file, not the output.

**Hand it on.** Could a stranger do the job from the map with the assistant off. Give a copy,
or share one place with rules on who changes what.

**Model swap.** Same folder, new model, maybe a new name for the map. Run it, do not assume
it.

**Gates are human checkboxes.** Read, never ticked by the assistant. No script drives the
stages.

**Leave with:** a folder that passed the walk test, or its failed items and their fixes.
